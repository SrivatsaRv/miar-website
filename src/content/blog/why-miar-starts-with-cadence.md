High resolution helps an analyst classify objects and inspect site detail. It does not, by itself, establish whether the observed activity is unusual. That requires another observation and a baseline chosen for the question being asked.

## A baseline is an analytical choice

The earliest available image is not automatically the right baseline. An analyst may need the previous pass, the same season last year, a known low-activity period, or several scenes that describe normal variation.

Baseline choice should be recorded with the comparison. Otherwise, two analysts can reach different conclusions from the same current image simply because they compared it with different points in time.

## Cadence should follow the question

There is no universally correct revisit interval. A question about aircraft arriving at a forward airfield may need close follow-up. A question about construction or long-term dispersal may be answered across weeks or months.

The useful cadence is the interval at which the activity can change enough to affect the assessment. More images are not automatically better if they do not change what the analyst can conclude.

> Collection frequency should be tied to the rate of change in the target activity.

## Counts need spatial context

In the Bholari example, the visible aircraft total can remain stable while the apron arrangement changes. A count-only system reports no difference. A comparison that retains object position and layout gives the analyst something to review.

The reverse is also true. A count may change because part of the site is obscured, outside the frame, or captured under different conditions. The scene and its quality remain part of the evidence.

## Normalize before drawing a trend

A time series built from imagery needs consistent classes and review rules. If one pass records “aircraft,” another records “transport,” and a third records an exact subtype, the resulting chart may imply a change that comes only from labelling.

The analyst also needs to account for scene coverage, resolution, viewing angle, and confidence. Trend points should link back to the image so any apparent rise or fall can be inspected.

## The useful output is a site history

A repeat-monitoring workflow should leave behind more than a folder of images. It should record which scene was reviewed, which baseline was used, what was observed, what changed, and whether an analyst accepted the finding.

- Scene date, source, and usable coverage
- Selected baseline and reason for selection
- Reviewed counts and classes
- Position, density, or layout observations
- Analyst disposition and confidence

Over time, that record allows a team to distinguish normal site behaviour from a sustained change. The value comes from the sequence and the review discipline, not from any single image alone.
